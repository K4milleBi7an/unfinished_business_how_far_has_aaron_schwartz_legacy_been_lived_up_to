# title: "Unfinished business: how far has Aaron Swartz's legacy been lived up to?"
# date: 2026-11-13
# categories: [digital-rights, open-access]
# tags: [aaron-swartz, open-access, cfaa, south-africa, ai]
# excerpt: "Swartz's legacy has been honoured unevenly: most fully where reform was cheap, partially where it needed the courts, and least where it challenges corporate power over information."

Aaron Swartz died on 11 January 2013, aged 26, while facing federal felony charges for downloading academic articles in bulk from the JSTOR archive through the Massachusetts Institute of Technology's network. He [faced a potential sentence of up to 35 years in prison](https://www.wyden.senate.gov/news/blog/post/wyden-and-logren-introduce-aarons-law-to-reform-the-cfaa). Since then, his name has become shorthand for a cluster of commitments: that publicly funded knowledge should be public, that computer-crime law should not turn civil disobedience into a life-altering felony, and that ordinary citizens acting together can defeat powerful interests, as in the [2012 protests against the SOPA and PIPA bills](https://www.eff.org/node/78304).

This post asks how far those commitments have been honoured. My answer is that the legacy has been lived up to unevenly: most fully where it asked institutions for reforms they could absorb at modest cost, only partially where it depended on courts rather than legislators, and least where it confronts concentrated corporate power over information, most visibly in the age of artificial intelligence (AI).

## What the legacy is, and what it is not

A legacy is easily sentimentalised, so it helps to be precise. Swartz was a technologist before he was an activist. As a teenager he contributed to the development of RSS, the syndication format [still used to distribute content online](https://theswaddle.com/aaron-swartz-who-championed-a-free-internet-died-by-suicide-10-years-ago-his-legacy-remains-vital), and he later built the web.py framework [while rewriting the software behind Reddit](https://tech.slashdot.org/story/26/11/07/0825245/). He [co-founded the campaigning organisation Demand Progress](https://demandprogress.org/?p=679451). His most radical statement of belief, the 2008 [Guerilla Open Access Manifesto](https://www.internazionale.it/notizie/alberto-puliafito/2024/03/31/intelligenza-artificiale-open-source), urged those with access to scholarly material to copy and share it beyond paywalls.

The legacy divides into three tests. The first is legal: has the law been reformed so that his prosecution could not recur? The second is scholarly: is publicly funded research now freely available? The third is civic: do the communities and tools he helped to create endure and still hold power to account?

## The legal test: a partial and judicial victory

The most direct response to his death was legislative. In 2013, Representative Zoe Lofgren and Senator Ron Wyden introduced ["Aaron's Law"](https://www.wyden.senate.gov/news/blog/post/wyden-and-logren-introduce-aarons-law-to-reform-the-cfaa), intended to refocus the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) on damaging intrusions and to stop prosecutors from inflating sentences by stacking overlapping charges. A [bipartisan, bicameral version followed in 2015](https://lofgren.house.gov/media/press-releases/lofgren-wyden-paul-introduce-bipartisan-bicameral-aaron-s-law-reform-computer) with Senator Rand Paul. Neither became law; the 2013 bill [stalled in committee and never reached the floor](https://searchcompliance.techtarget.com/definition/The-Computer-Fraud-and-Abuse-Act-CFAA). More than a decade on, the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) still describes Aaron's Law as [the starting point for sensible reform](https://www.eff.org/files/2025/08/19/rebooting_the_computer_fraud_and_abuse_act.pdf) and criticises the Act's harsh penalties for repeat offences, which can be used to pressure defendants into plea bargains.

The more consequential change came from the judiciary. In *Van Buren v. United States* (2021) the Supreme Court, by six votes to three, [narrowed the Act's reach](https://www.kaufcan.com/?p=13829) and clarified the meaning of "exceeds authorized access" ([EFF case page](https://www.eff.org/cases/van-buren-v-united-states)). Wyden, who helped write Aaron's Law, [welcomed the ruling](https://www.wyden.senate.gov/news/press-releases/wyden-statement-on-scotus-van-buren-v-united-states-decision) as a limit on prosecutorial overreach. This is a genuine gain, and it addresses the theory that made Swartz's case so frightening, namely that breaching an access policy might itself be a federal crime.

Yet judicial narrowing is a thinner inheritance than statutory reform. A decision interpreting one phrase does not address sentence stacking, the severity of penalties, or the discretion prosecutors retain, and it can be revisited by later courts. On the legal test, the verdict is partial: the worst interpretive risk has receded, but the legislature has not done what Swartz's death prompted it to promise.

## The scholarly test: the strongest success

On open access the record is considerably better. In February 2013, within weeks of his death, the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP) [directed agencies to develop plans](https://guides.lib.ku.edu/public_access) making federally funded publications free within a year of publication. In August 2022 the "Nelson memo" went further, [removing the embargo](https://www.researchprofessionalnews.com/?p=444725) and requiring agencies to make publications and supporting data publicly accessible on publication. Agencies were told to have updated policies in place [by 31 December 2025](https://casrai.org/news/federal-public-access-mandate-beyond-nih-2026), and the National Institutes of Health [brought its own policy forward to 1 July 2025](https://scholarworks.duke.edu/open-access/ostp-policy-guidance). In principle, taxpayer-funded American research is now free to read, the outcome Swartz argued the public had already paid for.

Three cautions apply. First, causation: the policy trajectory was shaped by years of advocacy by libraries, funders and researchers, and attributing it to Swartz alone would overreach, although his case undoubtedly sharpened its moral force. Second, durability: the Nelson memo has [reportedly been removed from the OSTP website](https://libguides.utsa.edu/federal_policies) under the current administration, and commentators describe the policy area as [still in motion rather than settled](https://casrai.org/news/federal-public-access-mandate-beyond-nih-2026). Third, scope and cost: the mandate covers publicly funded work, not scholarly publishing as a whole, and publishers have [signalled that they want funders to support paid "gold" open-access models](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/us-moves-to-make-federally-funded-research-free-upon-publication), which risks moving the barrier from the reader to the author. The scholarly test is the best-passed of the three, but not conclusively.

## The civic test: remembrance and continuity

The civic dimension of the legacy is the easiest to see and the hardest to evaluate. Each year on 8 November, [Aaron Swartz Day](https://aaronswartzday.org/) brings together developers and campaigners; in 2026 a hackathon was held at the Internet Archive and online, with Creative Commons participating and the [EFF promoting it](https://www.eff.org/node/112515). This year, when he would have turned forty, the Free Software Foundation used the occasion to [urge the companies that benefit from his work](https://tech.slashdot.org/story/26/11/07/0825245/) to give users genuine freedom and control rather than imposing further restrictions. [Demand Progress](https://demandprogress.org/?p=679451) continues to mark the anniversary and to carry out campaigns in his name. His technical work also persists: the whistleblower submission system now known as SecureDrop [began as code he wrote under the name DeadDrop](https://www.schneier.com/tag/aaron-swartz/).

These continuities are real, but remembrance is not the same as fulfilment. An annual hackathon honours the person, whereas the FSF's call is a reminder that firms built on the open web he helped shape do not always practise the openness he championed.

## The new test: artificial intelligence

That discomfort is now most visible in debates over AI. Writing in January 2026, Bruce Schneier and J. B. Branch [argued](https://www.schneier.com/essays/archives/2026/01/as-the-ai-arms-race-ramps-up-we-cant-let-big-tech-control-access-to-information.html) that the United States is still living inside the contradiction that destroyed Swartz: an individual was threatened with decades in prison for bulk-downloading research, while AI companies scrape data at industrial scale, often without consent, compensation or transparency, to train commercial models. Others make a related point about the meaning of "open": models that release their weights but not their training data [fall short of the openness Swartz had in mind](https://www.internazionale.it/notizie/alberto-puliafito/2024/03/31/intelligenza-artificiale-open-source).

The analogy deserves scrutiny, however. Swartz acted alone, publicly motivated, and accessed a subscription archive without authorisation; commercial AI developers are for-profit entities whose practices raise different questions about authors' consent and, arguably, about the burden bulk collection places on those who host content. Courts and legislatures have yet to settle those questions. The strongest version of the argument is therefore not that scraping should be criminalised, nor that it should be exempt, but that the standard applied should not depend on the power of the actor. Whether that principle is now being honoured is, at best, unresolved, and for that reason AI is the arena in which his legacy is most actively being tested.

## The South African dimension

Direct evidence of Swartz's influence in South Africa is thin, so the defensible claim is one of parallels rather than causes. The clearest direct response came in 2013, when a KwaZulu-Natal research institute [condemned his prosecution](https://krisp.org.za/blogs.php?id=20) and re-coded its publications page to widen distribution of its manuscripts and datasets, arguing that open access can save lives in developing nations. That was one institution's gesture, not a national shift.

The strongest parallel is open access. Cabinet approved a National Open Science Policy in December 2025, [requiring all outputs of publicly funded research to be open access](https://sparcopen.org/news/2026/south-africa-and-australia-embrace-promising-open-science-policies/), replacing a 2015 National Research Foundation statement that [allowed a 12-month embargo](https://www.academicjobs.com/higher-education-news/south-africa-open-access-policy-mandatory-for-public-research-or-academicjobs-14746). The groundwork was long, however: South Africa [joined the SciELO open-access programme in 2009](https://blog.mdpi.com/2024/10/29/open-access-in-south-africa), and advocates frame the movement around equity and social justice, with calls for a national fund for article processing charges.

The parallels in law are more cautionary. In 2015 the EFF [warned](https://kittens.eff.org/files/2015/11/30/submission_on_south_african_cybercrime_bill.pdf) that South Africa's draft cybercrimes bill treated access beyond written authority as unlawful, which could criminalise security researchers. The Cybercrimes Act now [criminalises unlawful access](https://cliffedekkerhofmeyr.com/en/news/publications/2020/technology/tmt-alert-7-july-The-Cybercrimes-Bill-is-one-step-away-from-becoming-law.html); whether it resolved that concern requires reading the final text. Finally, the Constitutional Court's June 2026 ruling [upheld the Copyright Amendment Bill's fair use model but left generative AI to Parliament](https://modeldiplomat.com/story/sa-court-upholds-fair-use-leaves-ai-to-parl), and [commentators already see fair use as relevant to AI training](https://bowmanslaw.com/?p=77098).

## Conclusion

The evidence suggests a legacy that is honoured selectively. Where reform was administratively simple and politically uncontroversial, as with public access to federally funded research, the outcome is closest to what Swartz sought. Where it demanded legislative courage, as with the CFAA, progress has come mainly through the courts and remains incomplete. Where it implicates the interests of the most powerful technology companies, the principle he embodied, that access to knowledge should not be governed by wealth, is contested rather than established. His death produced real institutional change and a durable community of memory; it has not yet produced a settled answer to the question he posed. The most faithful way to honour him, on this reading, is not to commemorate him annually but to keep the questions of who may access knowledge, who profits from it and who is punished for sharing it open.
